# CRM 2.0 Compliance — Voiceover Scripts

> **Usage:** These scripts are for recording screen walkthroughs of the CRM 2.0 compliance flow. Read them aloud while capturing the screen. Follow the [SCREEN] markers for what should be visible, pause at [PAUSE] markers to allow clicking and navigation, and give the [TIP] callouts extra emphasis.
>
> **How this set is built:** one **Main Walkthrough** takes a job from the start of the flow up to the Certificate review checkpoint. From there the job splits three ways, so there are three short branch scripts — **A: Satisfactory certificate**, **B: Required remedial works**, and **C: Optional remedial works**. After those come the **Shorts** — quick, snappy references for the situations staff hit most often.

---

## Main Walkthrough: CRM 2.0 Compliance — Start to Certificate Review

**Estimated Duration:** 8–10 minutes

---

[SCREEN: The CRM 2.0 requests queue]

Welcome to CRM 2.0 compliance. Before we touch a single job, understand the one idea that makes the whole system click. CRM 2.0 runs on two lanes. In the first lane, an AI agent runs the job from end to end — it chases the landlord, finds a contractor, books the visit, and sends the reminders. In the second lane, you, the operator, step in only for the decisions that need a human. Your whole job is to know when the system hands over to you, and to make the right call when it does.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The requests queue, highlighting the orange flag on a job]

This is your requests queue. Every compliance job lives here — the job ID, the property, the agency, the certificate type, and the status. Two things to know. Use the dropdown to filter to your Property Managers, and use "Hide cancelled" and "Hide completed" to cut the noise. And this — the orange flag — is the one that matters. An orange flag means the job has escalated and needs a human. For now, flagged jobs are the only ones you action.

[TIP] Don't go hunting through jobs that haven't escalated. If a job isn't flagged, the agent still has it. Leave it to work.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: A single compliance job open — the three areas of the screen visible]

Let's open a job. The screen has three areas. On the left is the activity panel — a full chronological history of everything the agent has done and every message to and from the parties. It reads like a messaging app: inbound messages on the left, outbound on the right. On the right is the property panel — the address, the certificate type, the contacts, and the list of planned actions. And along the bottom is the action area, where you'll find the buttons, including Take Over.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The AI Summary on the activity panel]

You'll see an AI Summary at the top of the activity. It's a quick read of where the job is and what happens next.

[TIP] Don't rely on the summary alone. 2.0 is new and the summaries are still maturing. Always read the job chronology so you're fully up to speed before you act.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The flow diagram, or a job showing its current stage]

Now let's walk the main path — the journey every job takes. There are six stages before completion, and the agent drives almost all of them. Stage one, Landlord approval. Stage two, Provider selection. Stage three, Scheduling. Stage four, Visit. Stage five, Result awaiting. And stage six, Certificate review — which is yours. Let's take them one at a time.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: A job at Landlord approval]

Stage one, Landlord approval. Every compliance request starts here. The agent asks the landlord to authorise the work before anything gets booked. It reminds them after one working day, then again at three days. If there's still no response by day four, the job auto-advances on its own to provider selection — and that includes EPCs. An unresponsive landlord doesn't stop an EPC renewal; the system assigns a provider and it proceeds, so the certificate is kept in date rather than left to lapse.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: A landlord message asking about price]

One thing to watch here — landlords often ask what the service costs before they'll approve. When that happens, check the price cheat sheet, reply to the landlord using Send Communication, and then click the approval button. Don't guess at a figure — read it straight from the sheet.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: A job at Provider selection]

Stage two, Provider selection. The agent picks the service provider and instructs them. It'll go with our preferred provider automatically. The only time it stops for you is when the landlord has asked for a specific contractor — then you steer it to the right one.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: A job at Scheduling]

Stage three, Scheduling. The agent contacts the tenant and the provider and finds a slot that works for both. It chases whichever side goes quiet and only escalates on a genuine deadlock. Most of the time you won't touch this stage at all.

[TIP] If you ever do need to log a date here — say a provider confirmed on a call — use Set Visit Date, not Add Direct Info. Set Visit Date is for recording a slot both sides have already agreed.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: A job at Visit]

Stage four, Visit. The appointment is booked. The agent holds it, sends confirmations, and reminds both sides the day before. Once the date passes, it moves the job on automatically.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: A job at Result awaiting]

Stage five, Result awaiting. The visit has happened and the agent is now waiting on the certificate from the provider. It chases at one day and two days, and escalates to you at three.

[TIP] Before you chase a provider yourself, check the last few emails on the job. The certificate is often already there — sometimes as a link rather than an attachment.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: A job arriving at Certificate review — the review panel visible]

And that brings us to stage six — Certificate review. This is your checkpoint. Every job stops here once a certificate has passed the agent's initial check, and it waits for your decision — there's no auto-timer pushing you along.

Whatever the certificate type, you check the same three things every time. The address — landlords sometimes send a certificate for another property in their portfolio, so confirm it's for this one. The numbers — subtotal, VAT and total. And the dates — the issue and expiry dates, present and accurate, in the right fields on both the certificate and the system.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The Certificate review panel with its buttons]

Once you've checked the document, what you do next depends on what the certificate shows. And that's where the job splits three ways. If the certificate is satisfactory, you approve it and the job heads for completion. If it shows faults that must be fixed, you send it down the required-remedial path. And if it's satisfactory but the engineer has noted optional improvements, you take the optional-remedial path.

The next three scripts cover those branches, one each. Pick the one that matches the certificate in front of you.

---

## Script A: Certificate Review — A Satisfactory Certificate

**Estimated Duration:** 3–4 minutes

---

[SCREEN: A satisfactory certificate open at Certificate review]

This is the most common outcome, and the simplest. The certificate is satisfactory — the property is compliant, and there are no remedial works to arrange. Let's confirm it properly.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: An EICR showing an overall "Satisfactory" outcome]

First, know how to read a pass on each certificate type. An EICR carries an overall outcome — Satisfactory or Unsatisfactory. It's satisfactory when there are no issues, or only C3 items, which are advisory. The codes that mean work is required are C1, C2 and FI. So if the outcome says Satisfactory and the only codes present are C3, you've got a clean pass.

[TIP] Read the codes, not just the summary box. FI — further investigation — is a fail code, just like C1 and C2. And if a certificate is marked satisfactory but still lists FI items, that's a contradiction — don't approve it as-is, go back to the service provider for clarification.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: A CP12 with the "Safe to use" column highlighted]

A gas certificate — a CP12 — works differently. It doesn't stamp the whole document satisfactory or unsatisfactory; it categorises each appliance. Read the "Safe to use" column. If every appliance says YES, there's no safety failure, and you treat the certificate as satisfactory.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The provider name field on the review panel]

Before you approve, one detail people get wrong — the provider name. If it's a Dwelly provider, copy and paste the name from the Contacts tab on the right. The agent sometimes pre-fills this field slightly differently from the name shown there, so copy the correct one across. If it's a landlord-DIY certificate, just use the name on the certificate itself.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: Approving the certificate — the job advancing to Invoice awaiting]

With the address, numbers, dates and provider name all checked, approve the certificate. The job now moves on to the invoice — Invoice awaiting, then Invoice review, which is your second checkpoint. There you'll check the subtotal, VAT and total add up and the reference is right, and then the job completes. On completion, the system serves the certificate to the tenant and the landlord automatically.

[TIP] If the invoice already arrived alongside the certificate, the job can skip straight from Certificate review to completion. That's expected — no extra step needed.

That's a satisfactory certificate, start to finish.

## Script B: Certificate Review — Required Remedial Works

**Estimated Duration:** 4–5 minutes

---

[SCREEN: An EICR showing C1 or C2 items]

This branch is for a certificate that has come back with faults that must be fixed for the property to be compliant. On an EICR, that's any C1, C2 or FI code. On a gas certificate, it's an appliance that isn't safe to use. When you see that, the works are required — not optional — and the job needs to go down the remedial path.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The Certificate review panel — the "Need required remedial" button]

Click Need required remedial. That takes the job off the main path and onto the remedial branch. Let's walk where it goes.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The remedial branch — Quotation awaiting]

First, Quotation awaiting. The agent goes back to the provider and asks for a quote for the extra works. You don't need to do anything while it waits.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The Quotation review panel]

Next, Quotation review — and this one's yours. Once the quote for the extra works arrives, check two things: the amount, and the description of the work. If it looks right, approve it.

[TIP] If the contractor hasn't sent a formal quotation document, just attach a screenshot of their quote to the job. That's an informal fix — there's no escalation point needed.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The job at Landlord quote approval]

Then, Landlord quote approval. The agent asks the landlord to agree to pay for the works. Usually there's nothing for you to do here — but if it escalates, record the outcome. If the landlord rejects the quote, call them and confirm whether they want to get their own quote or additional quotes from other agency contractors. If they want to use their own contractor, that contractor has to be vetted first. If they decline entirely, assert full landlord DIY.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The job re-entering Scheduling for the repeat visit]

If the landlord approves, the job re-enters Scheduling for a repeat visit to carry out the works — and from there it runs the normal path again. If the landlord rejects the quote and the works won't go ahead, the branch ends at Remedial terminated. Either way, the original certificate still stands as the compliance record.

That's the required-remedial branch — quote, review, landlord approval, repeat visit.

## Script C: Certificate Review — Optional Remedial Works

**Estimated Duration:** 3–4 minutes

---

[SCREEN: A satisfactory certificate that also lists C3 / recommended items]

This branch is for a certificate that passed — the property is compliant — but the engineer has noted some optional improvements. On an EICR, that's C3 items. On a gas certificate, it's a recommendation noted against an appliance that's still safe to use. The certificate is satisfactory; these extras are nice-to-have, not required for compliance.

[TIP] Don't automatically push an optional item down the required path. On an EICR it's clear-cut — C1, C2 and FI are required, C3 is optional. On a CP12 there's no overall pass or fail stamp, so "Safe to use: YES" means there's no safety failure forcing the works — but it doesn't make every noted item elective. Read the engineer's notes and judge each one on its own merits.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The Certificate review panel — the "Optional remedial" button]

When you've decided an item is genuinely optional, click Optional remedial. Now, here's the important part — and it's changed, so listen closely if you knew the old way.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The job moving onto the remedial branch — Quotation awaiting]

Optional remedials now follow the exact same path as required ones. Pressing Optional remedial sends the job down the same branch — Quotation awaiting, then Quotation review, then Landlord quote approval, then a repeat visit to do the works. It all stays inside CRM 2.0. There's no hand-off to a separate maintenance job in CRM 1.0 any more, and you don't send the landlord a separate comms email yourself.

[PAUSE]

[SCREEN: The satisfactory certificate saved to file on the job]

So what's the difference between optional and required, if they take the same path? Just one thing — and it's the whole point. Because the certificate already passed, the satisfactory certificate is saved to file. The property stays compliant whether or not the optional works ever go ahead. With a required remedial, compliance depends on the works being done; with an optional one, you're already compliant, and the works are a bonus on top.

[TIP] That's the mental model: same path, but an optional remedial banks the passing certificate first. A satisfactory certificate doesn't become unsatisfactory just because one optional item was noted.

That's the optional-remedial branch.

## Shorts — Quick Reference Guides

> **Usage:** Each short is a standalone 20–40 second clip for a single situation. Keep them fast and punchy — one problem, one answer, done.

---

### Short 1: A Visit Was Booked but the Appointment Didn't Go Ahead

[SCREEN: A job at Visit showing "Reset Visit Date" and "Reschedule"]

The visit didn't happen and you need to rebook. From the Visit stage — and also from Result awaiting — you've got two buttons, and picking the right one comes down to a single question: has a new time already been agreed?

If yes — both sides have confirmed a new slot in the thread, or one proposed it and you confirmed the other by phone — use Reset Visit Date. It updates the booking and notifies both sides automatically.

If no new time is agreed yet, use Reschedule. Choose whether to restart with the tenant or the provider, and write the opening message so the context carries over — something like "can't do tomorrow, next Monday works." Any dates you put in your message are recognised and carried through the flow.

[TIP] Same rule, every time: new time already agreed, Reset Visit Date. No time agreed yet, Reschedule.

---

### Short 2: A Date Was Agreed on a Call — Record It

[SCREEN: The Set Visit Date panel]

A provider or tenant confirmed a slot over the phone. This is the single most common mistake in the whole flow, so get it right: a visit date has to be agreed *and recorded in the system* before the job proceeds. It's enforced strictly.

Only use Set Visit Date once *everyone* has confirmed the same slot — in most cases that's both the tenant and the provider. It records an agreed appointment, not a proposal; if you're still passing dates between the two sides, that's Add Direct Info, not this. Once it's agreed, log it — Set Visit Date at the Scheduling stage, or Reset Visit Date if you're rescheduling.

Date and time are both required. If the slot is a window rather than an exact time — "in the morning", "8 to 10", "after 5" — put that in the Time note field. The time note overrides the exact time, so it's how you record a range.

[TIP] Notes like "with keys" only work *alongside* a time note — a note on its own leaves the job with no visit time at all. And if a date changes, record it in the system: a confirmation that only lives in your call notes doesn't count.

---

### Short 3: A Document Arrived Outside the Flow — via Slack or Direct Email

[SCREEN: The Add Direct Info panel with source "From Provider"]

A provider sent the certificate straight to you — on Slack, or by direct email — instead of through the normal channel. Don't take over the job to upload it.

Use Add Direct Info, set the source to From Provider, and attach the file. The agent then processes it exactly as if it had received it itself, and the job keeps flowing automatically.

[TIP] Taking over to upload it by hand pulls the job out of the automated lane unnecessarily. Add Direct Info keeps it running.

---

### Short 4: A Provider Is Away, or a Third Party Is Blocking the Job

[SCREEN: A job being diverted to Manual]

Two situations, one answer. First: "Wait for response" caps at three days — so if a provider is away longer than that, say on annual leave, don't just keep re-setting the wait. Second: if a job is blocked by someone who isn't the tenant, landlord or provider — a block manager, say — you need somewhere to park it.

For both, divert the job to Manual. Manual is the hold state in CRM 2.0. It lets the job sit outside the automated flow until things move again — use it instead of cancelling and re-creating the job.

[TIP] Manual is also where British Gas jobs go. If something needs to sit outside the flow for a while, that's what Manual is for.

---

### Short 5: Call Before You Wait

[SCREEN: A silent party on a job, with the Call button visible]

A party's gone quiet and you're tempted to just set Wait and move on. Don't — call them first.

Setting Wait without calling the silent party is the single most-flagged operator deviation. The process asks for a call, not just a snooze. Wait is for *after* you've tried to reach them.

[TIP] Call, then send a follow-up email — every time, including when you leave a voicemail — so they can find the right thread to reply to. Then, if you still need to, set Wait.

---

### Short 6: The Landlord Asks the Price Before Approving

[SCREEN: A landlord message asking about cost, at Landlord approval]

The landlord won't approve until they know the cost. Quick loop: check the price cheat sheet, reply with the figure using Send Communication, and then click the approval button once they're happy.

[TIP] Give the figure straight from the cheat sheet — don't guess. The sheet was built from the numbers the team supplied.

---

### Short 7: The Landlord Sent a Certificate for the Wrong Property

[SCREEN: A certificate for a different property in the landlord's portfolio]

The landlord has several properties with us and attached the wrong certificate. Two actions, not one — don't just bounce it back.

First, upload the certificate to the property it actually belongs to, through CRM 1's File Manager. It's a valid document for that property, so don't waste it. Second, separately, request the correct certificate for this job's property.

[TIP] The AI may also flag an address on a certificate. That's just a hint to look — not an error to fix — unless it really is a completely different property.

---

### Short 8: A Satisfactory Certificate That Lists FI Items

[SCREEN: An EICR marked satisfactory but showing FI codes]

You've got an EICR marked satisfactory, but it lists FI items — further investigation. That's a contradiction. FI is a fail code, so a genuine satisfactory certificate shouldn't carry one.

Don't approve it as it stands. Go back to the service provider and ask for clarification before you do anything else.

[TIP] Same instinct any time the summary box and the coded observations disagree — trust the codes, and get it clarified.

---

### Short 9: The Invoice Doesn't Match the Quote

[SCREEN: An invoice at Invoice review with a total that differs from the quote]

The invoice total doesn't match the quote. Don't reject it outright.

There are legitimate reasons the numbers differ — the provider found something extra on site, or the landlord or PM asked for additional work, or the provider simply quotes differently from how they invoice. So the default isn't rejection, it's a flag: raise the mismatch with the PM and let them make the call.

[TIP] This applies to compliance and maintenance alike. Flag it, don't auto-reject it.

---

### Short 10: A Relative Time Like "Morning" Isn't a Confirmed Slot

[SCREEN: A message saying "morning works" and the Set Visit Date panel]

Someone's confirmed a "morning" or an "afternoon" visit rather than an exact time. The agent won't accept a relative time as a confirmed slot, so it'll sit there waiting.

Step in and use Set Visit Date manually. Put the window straight into the Time note field — "in the morning", or "8 to 10" — which overrides the exact time. Or, if you'd rather pin it, treat morning as roughly 8am to 12pm. Either way records a firm slot and lets the job move on.

[TIP] Don't wait for the party to come back with an exact minute — set it manually and keep the job flowing.

---

### Short 11: A Vacant Property — the Day-Before Keys Check

[SCREEN: An Unattended job with a key-verification escalation]

The property's flagged Unattended — there's no tenant. The agent goes straight to the provider, explains that keys are collected from the agency, and books from the provider's availability.

The one thing that comes to you is the day before the visit: an escalation to verify the keys are actually available for collection. Check they're there, and clear it.

[TIP] Vacant doesn't mean hands-off. That day-before keys check is the human step that stops a wasted visit.
